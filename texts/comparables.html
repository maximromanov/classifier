Modeling types

Let’s try to model selected genres. Modeling here would mean two interconnected things. First, we would need to create a simplified representation of a more complex entity.1 Second, we would need to create a computational abstraction extracted from our representation that could then be used to assess other texts.

Thus, we may start with, using the pragmatic “comparables” approach, selecting several chronicles that would serve as a representation of the tawāriḫ type (historical texts). For example: Tajārib al-umam of Miskawayh (d. 421/1030), Ḏayl kitāb tajārib al-umam of Ẓahīr al-Dīn al-Rūḏrāwarī (d. 488/1096), al-Kāmil fī-taʾrīḫ of Ibn al-Aṯīr (d. 630/1233), al-Bayān al-muġrib of Ibn ʿIḏārī al-Marakushi (d. 725/1325), al-Muḫtaṣar fī aḫbār al-bašar of Abū-l-Fidāʾ (d. 732/1332), and al-ʿIbar fī-l-ḫabar of al-Ḏahabī (d. 748/1348).

Since texts are of varied length, we can break them into sections of 15,000 tokens. Breaking texts into chunks will give more samples that we can use in training (in the case of selected historical texts, we will have 371 samples). We can then randomly pull the same number of samples from each text type and use them for training our type-identification model. Altogether, we will be using 125 samples for each modeled type, of which about 100 will be used for training and the remaining 25 or so—for automatic evaluation of model performance. Selecting multiple texts and chunking them into samples will help us to ensure that our model does not look too much like a very specific text; chunking also helps to balance the volume of each text, by reducing the influence of longer texts and increasing the influence of shorter texts.

Altogether, for this experiment, I will use the following 10 types:

  1. [B] biographical texts (tarājim)—mostly collections of biographies;
  2. [E] exegetical texts (tafsīr);
  3. [G] Graeco-Arabic, “Greek-esque”, texts (yūnānīyāt) that revolve around translations from Greek and the development of Greek philosophy (falsafaŧ, predominantly Aristotelian);
  4. [H] historical texts (tawāriḫ)—mainly chronicles;
  5. [F] legal texts (fiqh)—here a broad variety of legal texts is included;
  6. [L] linguistic texts, broadly defined and including specimens of grammatical, rhetorical and lexicographical works (balāġaŧ, naḥw, maʿājim luġawiyyaŧ);
  7. [M] medical texts (ṭibb) are mainly titles on Galenic medicine (arguably, this category can be merged with “Greek-esque” texts);
  8. [P] poetic texts (šiʿr)—mainly collections of poetry (dīwāns);
  9. [Ḥ] Tradition-based texts, i.e. collections of ḥadīṯ;
  10. [K] “scholastic-theological” texts (kalām).

This list of 10 types is not meant to be complete. For example, some colleagues may be surprised not to find the adab type in the list. Unlike other types, adab is very difficult to define in this manner, primarily because it often includes other major types. Adab embodies a dynamic and multifaceted intellectual tradition that has shaped the cultural, ethical, and literary currents of the Islamic world. It serves as a blueprint of learned culture, outlining ideals of knowledge, conduct, and eloquence while adapting over time and space. Adab also functions as a performance script, guiding the enactment of these ideals in contexts ranging from courtly conversations to literary compositions, demanding active and graceful engagement. It is a toolkit of practical resources—an array of rhetorical, moral, and intellectual tools—and as an encyclopedia of knowledge that spans disciplines and genres, reflecting the values and tastes of its time. For all these reasons, our approach to adab in the context of machine learning should perhaps be different. Instead of trying to capture it like we can with other—definitely more definable—types, we can instead use the model trained on basic types to understand how adab texts work from within. With all this in mind, I have tried to exclude from the training data all the texts that are conventionally characterized as works of adab. This should allow us to see how they may interrelate with other types and whether those works have a common denominator that we can identify with this method or not. We will return to adab in one of the following sections, but I should emphasize that adab definitely requires a detailed and focused study of its own.

One can also argue that some of these types can be broken into separate types—for example, legal texts can be broken into multiple sub-types in a variety of different ways (for example, along the lines of uṣūl al-fiqh, furūʿ al-fiqh, and masāʾil, or along the lines of individual legal schools). Some other types, on the other hand, can be treated together as a single type. Thus, medical texts that are written within the Galenic tradition, can be arguably treated as a part of the broader Graeco-Arabic tradition. The same, perhaps, can be said about historical and biographical texts, which may also be treated as a single (meta?) type.

Overall, this model is offered here as a proof of concept as well as a roadmap for further, more detailed inquiry into the typology of the Arabic written tradition. All in all, in order to identify all stable and distinct types, one must design and conduct a complex long-term experimental study, where all possible types will be iteratively modeled and their stability repeatedly tested (in a manner similar to what we will do in the next section on periodization). In the end, one should be able to arrive to a stable set of types that remain more or less unchanged when we test them with a series of different models trained on different sets of training data. For now, however, our focus should remain on highlighting the kinds of questions that we can deal with using type-detection models.

What follows are the detailed lists of titles used for modeling each of these text types. Creating training data for any machine learning task is a complex endeavor and any training data can always be improved in a variety of ways. The quality of models and their performance depends directly on how good the training data is. For example, more titles can be added to increase the variety. Additionally, data can be manually balanced to ensure that larger texts—with more samples—are not overwhelming smaller texts with less samples. That said, training data selection can be also driven by different assumptions about any given type as well as research questions which one may be interested in answering. The current working selection should suffice for making the main point, but in general this should be understood only as a first step in modeling the typology of Arabic written tradition.
